# Diagnose with discriminating evidence

Read when investigating a failure, regression, or incident with an uncertain cause. Keep diagnostic experiments within the task's authorized effects. A delegated `hive-research` child gathers read-only evidence (logs, state, source, history); the main thread runs the experiments, because only it holds the task's authorized effects, and briefs the child with the evidence it needs next.

1. Establish expected and observed behavior, affected scope, actual revision/configuration, and recent relevant changes. Reproduce when feasible; otherwise preserve logs, traces, state, or other evidence and state the limits of the reconstruction. Failure to reproduce is not proof that the event did not occur.
   While the failure can still occur, form no hypothesis and edit no code until you can name a command or observation you have already run that shows the exact reported symptom and would show its absence once fixed; one that merely completes without error does not count. When you cannot build one, say what you tried and either reconstruct from preserved evidence as above or ask the user for an environment that reproduces it, a captured artifact, or permission for temporary instrumentation. During an incident, mitigation (step 2) does not wait for this.
   A forced failure, produced by changing code, a fixture, or data, only builds the reproduction and is not a fix; before trusting it, confirm with a diff against the unchanged copy that the change took effect. Once the failure reproduces, shrink it to the smallest reproduction that still shows the symptom, and compare it with the closest case that works: what differs between them is where to look. For an intermittent failure, raise how often it reproduces, such as by repeating it, adding load, or fixing a seed or the timing, before forming hypotheses, and report the rate you observed.
2. During an incident, prioritize reducing ongoing impact within existing authority while preserving useful evidence when practical. Mitigation may precede a complete causal explanation; distinguish temporary mitigation from a verified fix.
3. Form plausible hypotheses from the evidence and inspect the relevant component boundaries. Choose the smallest discriminating observation or experiment, name the expected result, and consider its risk before changing the system. Change one variable per experiment, so that a result points to one cause. Capture only necessary, redacted diagnostic data rather than dumping environment variables or payloads, and mark every temporary log line or probe you add with one unique tag. Without an authorized implementation, add probes and forced failures only in the working tree and in local fixtures, never in a shared or remote system.
4. Compare the result with the prediction and revise the explanation. Reconsider the approach when actions stop producing information or increase impact; fixed counts of fixes or deployments do not establish a useful stopping condition.
5. Verify the affected behavior and relevant regression after correction or mitigation. Separate observed recovery, supported cause, and remaining uncertainty; identify temporary measures that still need retirement. Before leaving the investigation, whether or not a correction was made, find the temporary instrumentation by searching for its tag and remove it, or list what remains as pending in the findings.
